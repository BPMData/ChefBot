## 1. Summary of Biggest Surprises and Disputes

### New England (`us_new_england`)
* **Northern vs. Southern New England divide:** Northern New England (Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont) is shaped by dense forests, dairy/maple farming, rocky cold-water coast, and Quebecois migration—leaning toward maple-sweetened baked beans, wild blueberries, apples/cider, game, *tourtiere* (French-Canadian meat pie), and plainly boiled lobster or cold mayonnaise lobster rolls. Southern New England (Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut) is denser, historically Puritan-mercantile and industrial-mill immigrant—anchored by molasses-and-salt-pork *Boston baked beans*, steamed brown bread, *Indian pudding*, Rhode Island clear-broth clam chowder, clam cakes, *stuffies*, white-corn *jonnycakes*, warm buttered Connecticut lobster rolls (credited to Perry's in Milford, CT, 1929), and the major Azorean/Madeiran/mainland Portuguese foodway of Fall River, New Bedford, and Providence (*chourico*, *linguica*, *massa de pimenta*, kale soup/*caldo verde*, *bacalhau*, *pao doce*).
* **Recommended app default:** Treat **everyday Yankee home cooking** (milk/cream-based fish or clam chowder, molasses-and-salt-pork baked beans, New England boiled dinner, apple/cranberry/maple baking) as the regional baseline, while explicitly tagging northern-tier (maple-sweetened, French-Canadian) and SouthCoast/RI Portuguese traditions as sub-regional tracks rather than erasing them.
* **Tourist food vs. home cooking:** Roadside clam shacks (fried whole-belly clams), $\$35+$ lobster rolls, commercial beach clambakes, and tavern Boston Cream Pie are seasonal tourist or special-occasion fare. Everyday home cooking is thrifty, winter-focused, and preservation-driven: pot roasts, boiled dinners, Saturday baked beans with canned or steamed brown bread, cheap whitefish chowders, *jonnycakes*, and apple crisp. Culinary historian Sandra Oliver has also debunked the viral claim that early American prisoners were routinely fed lobster as cruel punishment—picking meat from thousands of shells for institutions was logistically impractical, even when coastal lobsters were cheap enough for fertilizer.
* **Disputes:** New England vs. Rhode Island clam chowder (dairy-enriched vs. clear clam-broth); whether Worcestershire sauce belongs in classic chowder (used in some baked beans and modern chowder variants, omitted by strict traditionalists); molasses vs. maple syrup in baked beans (molasses in Boston/southern NE; maple in Vermont/Maine); whole-kernel vs. stone-ground white vs. yellow cornmeal in *jonnycakes*.

### Italian-American (`us_italian_american`)
* **A distinct American regional cuisine:** Centered on the five boroughs of NYC, Long Island, Westchester/Lower Hudson Valley, northern and central New Jersey, and Fairfield/New Haven counties in Connecticut, this cuisine was forged largely by the 1880–1924 wave of immigrants (>80% from Southern Italy—primarily Campania, Sicily, Calabria, Puglia, and Basilicata). Faced with abundant cheap meat, canned tomatoes, and dairy, cooks adapted southern Italian templates into Sunday gravy, chicken and eggplant parmigiana, baked ziti, stuffed shells, *braciole*, sausage and peppers, escarole and beans, neighborhood pork-store/*salumeria* charcuterie, bakery *sfogliatella* and *pignoli* cookies, the NY slice, New Haven *apizza*, and the Christmas Eve Feast of the Seven Fishes (*La Vigilia*—whose strict "seven" count solidified as an American ritual).
* **Local dialect and which words a recipe app should use:** Southern Italian dialects (especially Neapolitan) underwent apocope (dropping final unstressed vowels) and consonant voicing/lenition in the diaspora, producing *gabagool* (*capocollo*), *mozz* / *mutzadell* (*mozzarella*), *rigott* (*ricotta*), *pasta fazool* (*pasta e fagioli*), *ah-beetz* (*apizza*), and *macaroni* as a blanket term for all pasta shapes. **Gravy vs. sauce** remains fiercely disputed even inside NY/NJ families (many Campanian-rooted households reserve "gravy" for long-simmered meat-infused Sunday tomato sauce and "sauce" or marinara for meatless tomato). **App rule:** Use **standard culinary/supermarket names in ingredient lists** (`hot capocollo / capicola`, `low-moisture or fresh mozzarella`, `ricotta`, `rigatoni` or `ziti`) so cooks outside the NY metro can shop accurately, while preserving iconic local dish names (`Sunday gravy`, `New Haven white clam apizza`) in titles and cultural notes.
* **Boundary against "Italian (Any)":** *Sunday gravy*, *spaghetti and meatballs*, *chicken parmigiana*, *baked ziti*, *stuffed shells*, *pepperoni* (an American cured pizza salami—in Italy *peperoni* means bell peppers), *New York slice*, *New Haven apizza*, and the codified *Feast of the Seven Fishes* belong to this regional Italian-American node. Conversely, cream-based *fettuccine Alfredo* is a broader American restaurant adaptation of Roman *fettuccine al burro*, while *Parmigiano-Reggiano*, *Arborio rice*, and *Aceto Balsamico di Modena* originate in Northern Italy.

### Roman & Central Italy (`roman_central_italy`)
* **How it differs from Northern and Southern Italy:** Unlike Northern Italy, central cooking uses far less butter, rice, polenta, and cream, favoring olive oil, cured pork jowl (*guanciale*) or lard (*strutto*), sheep's-milk *pecorino*, and extruded durum pasta alongside handmade pastas. Unlike Southern & Sicilian Italy (Campania southward), it uses much less citrus, eggplant, capers, sweet-and-sour raisins/pine-nut pairings (except in specific Roman *coda alla vaccinara* and Jewish-Roman dishes), or pervasive chili heat (except in Abruzzo), leaning instead into pastoral mutton/lamb, Testaccio *quinto quarto* offal, Umbrian forest truffles, *norcineria* pork curing, and highland legumes.
* **Scope decision—Tuscany should be its own node (excluded from `roman_central_italy`):** Although Tuscany sits in central Italy and shares *cucina povera* and saltless bread (*pane sciapo*) with Umbria and inland Marche, leading authorities (Marcella Hazan, Gillian Riley) treat Tuscany as its own distinct culinary pillar defined by pungent green extra-virgin olive oil, Chianina beef (*bistecca alla fiorentina*), cannellini beans, *cavolo nero*, and bread soups (*ribollita*). By contrast, Lazio/Rome, Umbria, Abruzzo, and Marche are bound together by Apennine shepherding/transhumance, sheep's-milk pecorino, *guanciale* and pork *norcineria*, and shifting historical borders (for example, Amatrice was historically part of Abruzzo before moving to Lazio in 1927). Merging Tuscany into this node would let Florentine clichés overshadow Rome, Abruzzo, Umbria, and Marche.
* **Key surprises and disputes:**
  * **Pecorino Romano DOP is mostly Sardinian:** Under Consorzio Tutela Pecorino Romano DOP data, **92%** of Pecorino Romano DOP is produced in Sardinia, with only **8%** combined from Lazio and Grosseto (Tuscany).
  * **Abruzzo's border ambiguity:** Italy's statistical agency (ISTAT) groups Abruzzo with the *Mezzogiorno* for historical Kingdom of Naples reasons, yet culinarily Abruzzo's sheep transhumance, pecorino, *guanciale*, and Amatrice connection tie it closely to Lazio and Umbria.
  * **Recent invention of Carbonara:** *Pasta alla carbonara* is not ancient; documented recipes emerged in mid-20th-century post-WWII Rome, and using cream, garlic, onion, or smoked bacon is widely rejected in modern Roman canon (though mid-century recipes occasionally varied on *pancetta* vs. *guanciale* and whole eggs vs. yolks).

## 2. Fact-Check of Existing App Descriptions

### Existing Node: "New England Seafood"
* **"New England seafood cooking follows the cold Atlantic coast from Connecticut and Rhode Island through Massachusetts, New Hampshire and Maine, with ports, tidal flats and fishing towns central to the style."**
  * **Verdict: Correct** (for coastal seafood specifically, though incomplete as a representation of New England cooking as a whole, which also relies heavily on inland farming, dairy, maple, apples, and industrial mill-city immigrant traditions). *Sources: Sandra L. Oliver, Saltwater Foodways (1995); Keith Stavely & Kathleen Fitzgerald, America's Founding Food (2004).*
* **"Indigenous coastal foodways, English colonial cooking, maritime trade and later immigrant fishing communities all contributed."**
  * **Verdict: Correct.** Indigenous peoples (e.g., Wampanoag, Narragansett) contributed shellfish pit-roasting, maize, beans, and squash; English colonists adapted puddings, pies, and boiled meats; maritime trade brought Caribbean molasses and salt cod commerce; Portuguese (Azorean/Madeiran/Cape Verdean), Irish, French-Canadian, and Italian communities shaped coastal and mill-town kitchens. *Sources: Stavely & Fitzgerald (2004); Oliver (1995).*
* **"New England clam chowder, lobster rolls, clambakes and baked or fried cod/haddock are representative."**
  * **Verdict: Disputed / Partially Wrong as "everyday" food.** Clam chowder and baked/fried cod or haddock are genuinely representative of both home and commercial cooking, **but** lobster rolls (invented ~1929 at Perry's in Milford, CT) and commercial clambakes are primarily **roadside/tourist or special-occasion foods**, not everyday New England home cooking. *Sources: John Mariani (Encyclopedia of American Food and Drink); Stavely & Fitzgerald (2004).*
* **Classic flavors: `black pepper`, `bay leaf`, `thyme`, `parsley`, `chives`, `onion`, `celery`, `lemon`, `mustard`, `Worcestershire sauce`.**
  * **Verdict: Mostly Correct, with two Disputed items.** `black pepper`, `bay leaf`, `thyme`, `parsley`, `onion`, `celery`, and `lemon` are core aromatics in coastal fish and chowder cookery. `chives` are common as a garnish but secondary. `mustard` (especially dry mustard) is authentic to baked beans and cheese/fish sauces, though not universal in plain chowder. **`Worcestershire sauce` is Disputed:** some modern New England baked-bean and chowder cooks add a dash for umami, whereas purists (and classic 19th-century/Fannie Farmer chowders) omit it entirely. It also leaves out core New England everyday seasonings such as **salt pork fat**, **summer savory**, **molasses**, **cider vinegar**, and **massa de pimenta** (in SE MA/RI). *Sources: Fannie Farmer, The Boston Cooking-School Cook Book (1896); Jasper White, 50 Chowders (2000).*
* **Signature ingredients:**
  * `quahogs/hard clams (substitute: littleneck or canned clams for chowder)` — **Correct / Nuanced:** Quahogs (*Mercenaria mercenaria*) are large hard clams; littlenecks are small clams of the exact same species (good substitute if chopped, though pricier), while canned clams work for chowder with a softer/chewier texture trade-off. *Source: Jasper White, 50 Chowders (2000).*
  * `Maine/New England lobster (substitute: other cold-water lobster; shrimp only changes the dish substantially)` — **Correct.** *Source: Oliver (1995).*
  * `salt cod (substitute: fresh cod plus added salt only for some preparations)` — **Wrong / Misleading.** Salt cod (*baccalhau* / *bacalao*) is both heavily salted and dried, giving it a firm, fibrous, concentrated cured profile that fresh cod plus salt cannot replicate in classic salt-cod cakes, *bacalhau*, or creamed salt cod; fresh cod works only as a different (fresh-fish) preparation. *Sources: Oliver (1995); White (1989).*
  * `haddock (substitute: cod or pollock)` — **Correct.** Atlantic cod, haddock, and pollock are standard interchangeable whitefish in New England chowders and baked/fried fish. *Source: White (1989, 2000).*

### Existing Node: "Italian" (International Image)
* **"This node deliberately represents the internationally recognized image of Italian food rather than a geographic region: pizza shops, red-sauce restaurants and diaspora kitchens made pasta, tomato sauce, garlic, basil and grated cheese global symbols of Italy."**
  * **Verdict: Correct** as a meta-description of the global stereotype/diaspora construct. *Source: John Mariani, How Italian Food Conquered the World (2011); Simone Cinotto, The Italian American Table (2013).*
* **"Much of this image was shaped by Italian migration to the Americas and later tourism and restaurant culture, so several famous dishes are export or Italian-American forms rather than everyday food across Italy."**
  * **Verdict: Correct.** *Sources: Cinotto (2013); Mariani (2011).*
* **"Representative dishes are spaghetti with meatballs, cream-style fettuccine Alfredo, pepperoni pizza and lasagna."**
  * **Verdict: Correct as examples of the international/export image, with key distinctions:** Spaghetti with giant meatballs and pepperoni pizza are **Italian-American inventions** (in Italy *polpette* are usually smaller and served separately, except Abruzzo's tiny *pallottine*; *peperoni* means bell peppers). Cream-style *fettuccine Alfredo* is an **American restaurant adaptation** of Roman *fettuccine al burro* (which uses butter and Parmigiano without cream). *Lasagna* (as *lasagne al forno* / *vincisgrassi*) genuinely exists across Italy (Emilia-Romagna, Marche, Naples), though the ricotta-and-mozzarella baked style is Southern Italian/Italian-American. *Sources: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/is-spaghetti-and-meatballs-italian-7487216/ ; Gillian Riley, The Oxford Companion to Italian Food (2007).*
* **Classic flavors: `garlic (aglio)`, `basil (basilico)`, `oregano (origano)`, `red chile flakes (peperoncino)`, `parsley (prezzemolo)`, `black pepper (pepe nero)`, `olive oil (olio d'oliva)`, `balsamic vinegar (aceto balsamico)`, `anchovy (acciuga)`, `rosemary (rosmarino)`.**
  * **Verdict: Disputed / Overgeneralized for Italy as a whole, mostly Correct as the international stereotype:**
    * `garlic`, `olive oil`, `parsley`, `black pepper`, `rosemary`, and `basil` (summer/central-south) are broadly authentic across Italy, though northern cooking also relies heavily on butter and sage.
    * `oregano` and `peperoncino` are primarily **Southern Italian and pizza/Italian-American** staples rather than pan-Italian everyday seasonings.
    * **`balsamic vinegar (aceto balsamico)` is Wrong as an everyday pan-Italian seasoning:** traditional *Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale* belongs to Modena and Reggio Emilia (Emilia-Romagna) and is used sparingly by the drop; everyday salad dressing across Italy uses red/white wine vinegar, and cheap "balsamic vinaigrette" is an international restaurant habit. *Source: Riley (2007).*
* **Dessert-only flavors: `vanilla (vaniglia)`.**
  * **Verdict: Correct** (for desserts such as *panna cotta*, *crema pasticcera*, and pastries, though citrus zest, almond, cinnamon, and coffee are equally common across Italian desserts). *Source: Riley (2007).*
* **Signature ingredients:**
  * `Parmigiano-style hard cheese (substitute: supermarket Parmesan)` — **Correct** for the international node, though in Southern Italy and Rome **Pecorino** (sheep's milk) is often the default grating cheese rather than Parmigiano-Reggiano (which is from Emilia-Romagna). *Source: Riley (2007).*
  * `fresh mozzarella (substitute: low-moisture mozzarella for baked dishes)` — **Correct** (for Italian-American baking, low-moisture whole-milk mozzarella is actually preferred over watery fresh mozzarella so pizzas and baked ziti do not weep). *Source: Mariani (2011).*
  * `prosciutto (substitute: thinly sliced cured ham)` — **Correct** (noting that US generic "cured ham" is usually wet-cured/smoked, unlike dry-cured *prosciutto crudo*).
  * `pancetta (substitute: unsmoked bacon if available; ordinary bacon is smokier)` — **Correct.**
  * `Arborio rice (substitute: another short-grain rice)` — **Correct** (noting Arborio is specifically **Northern Italian**—Piedmont/Lombardy—not Southern/diaspora). *Source: Riley (2007).*
