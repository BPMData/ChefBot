### Summary of Surprises and Disputes

1. **New England (`us_new_england`): Northern vs. Southern Divide, App Default, and Tourist vs. Home Food**
   - **Northern vs. Southern New England:** Northern New England (Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont) has shorter growing seasons, dense forests, and strong French-Canadian/Acadian influences (*tourtière*, *cretons*, wild blueberries, maple syrup, artisan dairy, and cold-water lobster). Southern New England (Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut) developed earlier urban-industrial hubs and distinct coastal traditions: Boston's molasses-sweetened Saturday baked beans and steamed brown bread, Rhode Island's stone-ground white flint-corn johnnycakes and clear-broth clam chowder, and a deep 19th–20th-century Portuguese maritime imprint in southeastern Massachusetts (New Bedford, Fall River) and Rhode Island (East Providence, Bristol) built around *chouriço*, garlic, paprika, kale soup (*caldo verde*), and stuffed quahogs ("stuffies").
   - **App Default:** Treat everyday southern/central New England home cooking as the baseline—creamy (or milk-based) fish/clam chowder, New England boiled dinner, Saturday baked beans and brown bread, baked scrod/haddock, roast poultry with savory herb stuffing, and apple/cranberry/maple desserts—while explicitly offering Rhode Island clear-broth chowder, Portuguese-New England dishes, and northern maple/dairy/French-Canadian staples as labeled sub-regional options.
   - **Clam-Shack vs. Home Cooking:** Deep-fried whole-belly clams, buttered/mayo lobster rolls in split-top buns, and seaweed-steamed pit clambakes are primarily commercial clam-shack, lobster-pound, or special-occasion tourist traditions. Everyday home cooking is far more reliant on one-pot boiled dinners, baked beans, fish chowders, pan-fried fish cakes, and simple baked or broiled white fish.

2. **Italian-American (`us_italian_american`): Local Lexicon and Scope vs. `Italian (Any)`**
   - **Gravy vs. Sauce Dispute:** Within the NYC tri-state area (the five boroughs, Long Island, Westchester/lower Hudson Valley, northern/central NJ, and Fairfield/New Haven counties in CT), calling slow-simmered meat-enriched tomato sauce *"Sunday gravy"* versus *"sauce"* is a longstanding family and sub-regional divide. Generally, Neapolitan- and Southern-descended households in Brooklyn, Staten Island, and northern NJ call a pot simmered with pork neck bones, sausage, meatballs, and *braciole* **"gravy"**, reserving **"sauce"** (or *marinara*) for quick meatless tomato preparations; others—especially families with northern Italian ties—use *"sauce"* for everything.
   - **Which Words Recipes Should Use:** Recipes should use standard, shoppable English/Italian-American ingredient names (*pasta* with the specific shape, *fresh mozzarella* or *low-moisture mozzarella*, *capocollo*, *ricotta*, *Sunday gravy / meat-simmered tomato sauce*) so cooks can actually buy them, while noting dialect forms (*macaroni* for any pasta, *mozz*/*mootz*, *gabagool*, *rigott'*, *apizza* pronounced "ah-beetz") in dish titles or cultural notes.
   - **What Belongs Here vs. `Italian (Any)`:** The app's `Italian (Any)` node holds the generic globalized restaurant image (spaghetti with meatballs, cream-style fettuccine Alfredo, pepperoni pizza, generic lasagna). `us_italian_american` is a distinct regional American cuisine shaped by immigrants from Campania, Sicily, Calabria, Puglia, and Basilicata adapting to abundant American beef, pork, wheat, and canned tomatoes: Sunday gravy with *braciole* and pork bones, pork-store salumeria and bakery culture, chicken and eggplant parmigiana, baked ziti, stuffed shells, sausage and peppers, escarole and beans, the foldable NY slice versus New Haven coal-fired *apizza* (plain tomato pie and white clam pie), and the Christmas Eve Feast of the Seven Fishes (*La Vigilia*—an Italian-American framing of Southern Italian meatless vigils).

3. **Roman & Central Italy (`roman_central_italy`): Differences from North/South and the Tuscany Question**
   - **How It Differs from Northern and Southern Italy:** Unlike Northern Italy's butter, risotto, polenta, and cream/wine braises, Central Italy relies on olive oil, rendered pork cheek fat (*guanciale*), sheep's milk *Pecorino*, and mountain pastoralism. Unlike Southern Italy & Sicily (already researched), it uses far less citrus, eggplant, sweet-and-sour *agrodolce*, or ubiquitous dried-pasta-with-tomato, favoring instead Rome's *quinto quarto* (fifth-quarter offal), egg-and-cheese emulsions, Umbrian *norcineria* (pork butchery) and truffles, and Abruzzese shepherd mutton (*arrosticini*) and mountain saffron.
   - **Should Tuscany Belong Here or Be Its Own Node?** **Tuscany should be its own separate node** rather than folded into `roman_central_italy`. Although geographically central, Tuscany's culinary grammar is markedly distinct: saltless bread (*pane sciocco*) and stale-bread soups/salads (*ribollita*, *pappa al pomodoro*, *panzanella*), *Chianina* beef (*bistecca alla fiorentina*), cannellini beans (*mangiafagioli*), olive oil over pork fat, and sage/rosemary estate *cucina povera*—whereas Lazio, Umbria, Abruzzo, and Marche share a tighter pastoral, *pecorino*, *norcineria*, and mountain-Apennine core. *(LOW CONFIDENCE on exact boundary placement for coastal Marche, which shares Adriatic fish stews like brodetto with Emilia-Romagna/Romagna.)*

### Fact-Check of Existing App Nodes

#### Node 1: `New England Seafood`
* **Description:**
  * *"New England seafood cooking follows the cold Atlantic coast from Connecticut and Rhode Island through Massachusetts, New Hampshire and Maine, with ports, tidal flats and fishing towns central to the style."* — **Correct** (Sandra L. Oliver, *Saltwater Foodways*, 1995; Mark Kurlansky, *Cod*, 1997).
  * *"Indigenous coastal foodways, English colonial cooking, maritime trade and later immigrant fishing communities all contributed."* — **Correct** (Indigenous clam/corn traditions, English stews, Triangular Trade salt cod/molasses, and Portuguese/French-Canadian fishing communities; Oliver, 1995).
  * *"New England clam chowder, lobster rolls, clambakes and baked or fried cod/haddock are representative."* — **Disputed** (Representative of coastal/tourist seafood, though lobster rolls and pit clambakes are commercial/special-occasion foods rather than everyday home cooking, and Rhode Island's traditional clam chowder is clear-broth rather than creamy Massachusetts style).
* **Classic flavors:**
  * `black pepper`, `bay leaf`, `thyme`, `parsley`, `chives`, `onion`, `celery`, `lemon` — **Correct** (core Anglo-Yankee seafood aromatics).
  * `mustard` — **Disputed** (appears in some tartar sauces, crab/lobster dishes, or boiled-dinner condiments, but is not a primary everyday seasoning across New England seafood).
  * `Worcestershire sauce` — **Disputed** (used by some modern home cooks and restaurants to boost umami in clam chowder or oyster cocktails, but purist/historic New England chowder recipes omit it).
* **Signature ingredients:**
  * `quahogs/hard clams` (sub: littleneck or canned clams for chowder) — **Correct** (littlenecks are juvenile quahogs, *Mercenaria mercenaria*; canned chopped clams work for chowder).
  * `Maine/New England lobster` (sub: other cold-water lobster; shrimp only changes the dish substantially) — **Correct**.
  * `salt cod` (sub: fresh cod plus added salt only for some preparations) — **Wrong** (salted fresh cod lacks the firm, flaky, cured texture and aged flavor of true dried salt cod/*bacalhau* required for fish cakes, creamed salt cod, or Portuguese dishes).
  * `haddock` (sub: cod or pollock) — **Correct** (Atlantic cod and pollock are standard New England substitutes for haddock).

#### Node 2: `Italian`
* **Description:**
  * *"This node deliberately represents the internationally recognized image of Italian food rather than a geographic region: pizza shops, red-sauce restaurants and diaspora kitchens made pasta, tomato sauce, garlic, basil and grated cheese global symbols of Italy."* — **Correct** (Simone Cinotto, *The Italian American Table*, 2013; John Mariani, *How Italian Food Conquered the World*, 2011).
  * *"Much of this image was shaped by Italian migration to the Americas and later tourism and restaurant culture, so several famous dishes are export or Italian-American forms rather than everyday food across Italy."* — **Correct** (Cinotto, 2013; Mariani, 2011).
  * *"Representative dishes are spaghetti with meatballs, cream-style fettuccine Alfredo, pepperoni pizza and lasagna."* — **Correct** as symbols of the international/export image (noting that *lasagna* also has deep authentic regional roots in Italy, such as Emilia-Romagna and Naples, whereas spaghetti with meatballs, cream-based Alfredo, and pepperoni pizza are American/export creations).
* **Classic flavors:**
  * `garlic (aglio)`, `basil (basilico)`, `oregano (origano)`, `red chile flakes (peperoncino)`, `parsley (prezzemolo)`, `black pepper (pepe nero)`, `olive oil (olio d'oliva)`, `anchovy (acciuga)`, `rosemary (rosmarino)` — **Correct** for the international/Italian-American pantry (though in Italy, dried oregano is heavily southern/Sicilian and rarely used in northern or most Roman pasta dishes).
  * `balsamic vinegar (aceto balsamico)` — **Disputed** (traditional *Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale* is a localized luxury condiment from Modena and Reggio Emilia in Emilia-Romagna, whereas industrial supermarket balsamic vinegar became a global "Italian" salad-dressing cliché rather than a staple of Southern Italian or Italian-American red-sauce cooking).
* **Dessert-only flavors:**
  * `vanilla (vaniglia)` — **Correct** (standard in both Italian *pasticceria* and Italian-American desserts).
* **Signature ingredients:**
  * `Parmigiano-style hard cheese` (sub: supermarket Parmesan) — **Correct** (supermarket Parmesan is an acceptable budget stand-in, though much milder and less crystalline than aged Parmigiano-Reggiano DOP).
  * `fresh mozzarella` (sub: low-moisture mozzarella for baked dishes) — **Correct** (low-moisture mozzarella is actually preferred in NY-style pizza and baked pasta).
  * `prosciutto` (sub: thinly sliced cured ham) — **Disputed** (typical US supermarket "cured ham" is wet-cured/cooked deli ham, which does not substitute for dry-cured *prosciutto crudo*; dry-cured Serrano or speck are closer).
  * `pancetta` (sub: unsmoked bacon if available; ordinary bacon is smokier) — **Correct**.
  * `Arborio rice` (sub: another short-grain rice) — **Correct** (Carnaroli or Vialone Nano are better Italian risotto matches; generic short-grain rice works in a pinch).
