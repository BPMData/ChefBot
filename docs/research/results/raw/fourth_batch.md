## Australian (`australian`)
- **Subtitle:** Coastal seafood, bush herbs, multicultural pantry
- **Summary & Disputes:** Modern Australian home cooking centers on coastal seafood, outdoor grilling, British-Irish colonial baking (self-raising flour, golden syrup, damper, ANZAC biscuits), post-war Mediterranean olive-oil-and-herb staples, and 1970s-onward Southeast and East Asian aromatics (fish sauce, soy sauce, lemongrass, laksa), accented sparingly with native botanicals such as lemon myrtle, finger lime, Tasmanian pepperberry, and wattleseed. Culinary historians Michael Symons (1982) and Barbara Santich (2012) offer contrasting interpretations—critiquing early reliance on industrial convenience foods versus celebrating colonial domestic improvisation—while Helen Leach's archival study (2008) shows New Zealand holds the earliest concentration of printed pavlova recipes (21 by 1940) despite ongoing trans-Tasman rivalry, alongside lively state-level naming debates (such as chicken 'parmi' vs. 'parma') and Indigenous intellectual-property concerns around commercialized bush foods. Sodium and salt figures for commercial chicken salt, brewer's yeast extract spreads, jarred laksa paste, and bottled sweet chilli and Australian-style tomato sauces are marked LOW CONFIDENCE because formulations vary substantially across brands and chicken-free variants.
- **Pantry (9):** extra-virgin olive oil, butter, self-raising flour, rolled oats and rice, garlic and brown (yellow) onions, lemons, soy sauce and fish sauce, Worcestershire sauce and sweet chilli sauce, golden syrup

## New Zealander (`new_zealander`)
- **Subtitle:** Lamb, mussels, kūmara, hāngī, baking
- **Summary & Disputes:** Aotearoa New Zealand cuisine joins Polynesian-adapted Māori foodways—kūmara horticulture, kaimoana coastal shellfish (green-lipped mussels, pāua, kina), seasonal whitebait, hāngī earth-oven steaming, mānuka wood smoking, native bush seasonings (horopito, kawakawa), and post-contact pork-bone boil-up and rēwena potato-starter bread—with British-derived lamb roasting, pasture dairy, tin-filling home baking (pavlova, ANZAC biscuits, ginger crunch), Central Otago stone fruit, backyard feijoas, and growing Pacific Island and Asian influences. Two recurring debates appear in culinary scholarship and local food writing: the trans-Tasman origin dispute between New Zealand and Australia over the pavlova (where Helen Leach traced printed New Zealand pavlova recipes back to the late 1920s alongside competing Australian claims), and ongoing discussion over how widely revived native bush ingredients like horopito and pikopiko appear in everyday Kiwi home cooking compared with restaurant menus, alongside South Island versus North Island regional loyalties (such as Southland cheese rolls and blue cod). ## West & Central India (`central_west_india`)
- **Subtitle:** Millets, kokum, jaggery, roasted masalas
- **Summary & Disputes:** West and Central India bridges arid interior millet-and-pulse cooking (jowar, bajra, besan, dairy, sun-dried pods and lentil nuggets) with Arabian Sea coastal coconut, kokum, and seafood traditions, linked across much of the region by mustard-seed and asafoetida tempering, peanut or sesame fats, and deliberate sweet-sour-fiery contrasts using jaggery with tamarind, kokum, buttermilk, or Goan toddy vinegar. It differs sharply from 'north_india' (where Indo-Gangetic wheat, cream, paneer, tandoori cooking, and Mughlai-Punjabi dishes dominate and black mustard seed is uncommon) and 'south_india' (built around fermented rice-urad batters and widespread coconut-curry-leaf bases), while 'india_any' captures Punjabi-Mughlai export restaurant tropes rather than this region's everyday meals (aside from heavily altered restaurant vindaloo). Major regional and scholarly debates concern Rajasthan—which ChefBot's 'north_india' entry currently lists for its wheat, ghee, dairy, and royal Rajput meat curries, yet shares Thar-desert preservation and Jain/Vaishnava no-onion, no-garlic foodways closely with Gujarat—as well as Madhya Pradesh's role as a central crossroads connecting Rajasthani baati to Malwa bafla and Maharashtrian poha, and Goa's internal divide between Konkan Saraswat Hindu coconut-kokum cooking and Goan Catholic Portuguese-influenced vinegar-and-pork traditions. Sodium estimates for commercial goda, kala, Malvani, pav bhaji, and undhiyu blends and jarred Goan vindaloo or recheado pastes are marked LOW CONFIDENCE because manufacturers vary widely in whether and how much salt they add.
- **Pantry (10):** peanut or neutral oil, and ghee, whole-wheat flour (atta) and chickpea flour (besan), millet flours (jowar, bajra) and rice, dried dals (toor, moong, chana), onions, garlic and fresh ginger, turmeric, cumin, coriander and dried red chiles, mustard seeds, curry leaves and asafoetida (hing), jaggery (gur / gul) and tamarind or kokum, roasted peanuts and sesame seeds, plain yogurt or buttermilk
- **Sources:**

## Venezuelan (`venezuelan`)
- **Subtitle:** Ají dulce, masarepa, papelón, queso blanco
- **Summary & Disputes:** Venezuelan home cooking blends Indigenous maize and cassava foodways with Spanish and African traditions across Caribbean coastal, llanos, and Andean ecosystems, relying on gentle slow-sweated sofrito with non-spicy ají dulce, annatto-tinted oil, papelón, precooked masarepa, and diverse fresh and aged white cheeses. Major regional and culinary disputes include whether caraotas negras in pabellón criollo should be lightly sweetened with papelón or sugar (the classic Caracas and eastern style codified by Armando Scannone) versus kept strictly savory (common in the Andes and Zulia), and whether hallaca meat fillings (guisos) are cooked beforehand as in Caracas or marinated raw and steamed inside the masa as in the Andes. Culinary historians including José Rafael Lovera, Rafael Cartay, and Ocarina Castillo also emphasize that the arepa reflects shared pre-Columbian maize-grinding traditions predating the modern borders of Venezuela and Colombia, with the term erepa reflecting an early Cumanagoto linguistic record rather than exclusive national invention. Brand-varying sodium and salt-equivalent figures for commercial adobo blends, bouillon cubes (cubitos), sazón packets, salsa inglesa, and jarred sofrito pastes are marked LOW CONFIDENCE because manufacturers vary significantly in salt, MSG, and soy or anchovy levels.
- **Pantry (10):** neutral vegetable oil, precooked white cornmeal (masarepa), long-grain white rice, dried black beans (caraotas negras), unrefined cane sugar (papelón), annatto seeds (onoto), garlic and yellow onions, cumin and dried oregano, Worcestershire-style sauce (salsa inglesa) and white vinegar, limes
- **Sources:**

  - Diccionario de alimentación y gastronomía en Venezuela, Rafael Cartay and Elvira Ablan, 1997

## Chilean (`chilean`)
- **Subtitle:** Merkén, cumin, pebre, Pacific seafood
- **Summary & Disputes:** Chilean culinary historians and anthropologists (including Eugenio Pereira Salas, Oreste Plath, and Sonia Montecino) frame Chilean food not as a single monolith but as plural regional 'cocinas chilenas' forged through Indigenous-Spanish mestizaje and 19th-century European influences—spanning Andean grain-and-camelid stews in the north, Central Valley bean, squash, and corn cookery, Chiloé's potato-and-shellfish pit traditions, and Patagonian open-fire lamb. Key disputes center on whether traditional empanadas de pino should include raisins (historically standard for sweet-savory contrast, now widely rejected by many home cooks) and pitted versus whole olives, whether classic pebre includes diced tomato or relies purely on cilantro, onion, garlic, and chili, and whether merkén is blended with salt and coriander or left salt-free. Commercial salt estimates for salted merkén, jarred pasta de ají, bouillon cubes, and bottled chancaca syrups vary considerably across producers and are marked LOW CONFIDENCE.

## The Guianas (`guianas`)
- **Subtitle:** Cassareep, masala, kecap manis, culantro
- **Summary & Disputes:** Research across regional institutional cookbooks and food-studies scholarship shows the Guianas form a distinct mainland South American-Caribbean culinary region anchored by Indigenous bitter-cassava detoxification (cassareep and couac), Afro-Creole and Maroon stews, Indo-Caribbean roasted masalas and dalpuri, Javanese-Surinamese kecap manis, trassi, and pindasambal, Chinese-Surinamese stir-fries, and French Guianese bouillon d'awara and colombo. Key regional and scholarly disputes include purists criticizing commercial bottled cassareep that relies on added brown sugar, caramel coloring, and chemical preservatives rather than long-boiled pure bitter-cassava juice, as well as the distinction between Guyanese/Surinamese roasted-cumin masala and French Guianese poudre de colombo (which incorporates toasted raw rice and clove) and between pomtayer (Xanthosoma) and true taro (Colocasia) in Surinamese pom. Exact sodium and salt levels for store-bought green seasoning jars, jarred masala/bumbu/sambal pastes, and non-traditional bottled cassareep vary substantially by manufacturer and are marked LOW CONFIDENCE.

## Yemeni (`yemeni`)
- **Subtitle:** Hawaij, fenugreek foam, zhoug, flatbreads
- **Summary & Disputes:** Authoritative historical, anthropological, and cookbook sources establish Yemeni cooking as sharply distinct from Levantine and Gulf Arab cuisines, anchored by two spice-blend families (savory and coffee hawaij), soaked and whipped fenugreek foam (hulba), mortar-crushed sahawiq (zhug), soapstone-pot stews (saltah, fahsa), smoked clarified butter (samn), and fermented or laminated breads (lahooh, malawah). Major regional and scholarly distinctions include highland sorghum, wheat, and stone-pot traditions around Sana'a versus Indian Ocean-influenced Adeni cooking (zhurbian with yogurt, fried onions, and potatoes, plus Adani chai), Hadhrami pit-roasted mandi, haneeth, Sidr honey, and cured shark (lakhm), and Tihama sorghum and coastal seafood with dried sardines (wazif). Disputed or diverging traditions center on diaspora evolution versus mainland practice: savory hawaij ratios vary between Sana'a, Aden, and family kitchens, and Yemenite-Jewish diaspora versions (malawach, skhug) often pan-fry laminated dough with margarine and serve grated tomato and boiled egg, whereas mainland Yemeni malawah is traditionally ghee-laminated and often baked in a clay tannur. Sodium estimates for commercial zhoug pastes, salted versus unsalted commercial hawaij blends, and commercial bouillon shortcuts are marked LOW CONFIDENCE because salt levels vary substantially by producer.
- **Pantry (10):** basmati rice, all-purpose and whole-wheat or sorghum flour, ghee (samn) or neutral oil, onions and garlic, tomato paste, savory hawaij spice blend, ground fenugreek seeds (hulba), chili-herb paste (sahawiq or zhoug), dried fava beans and lentils, plain yogurt and lemons or limes
- **Sources:**

## South African (`south_african`)
- **Subtitle:** Pap, braai, Cape Malay, Durban curry
- **Summary & Disputes:** South African home cooking weaves four major culinary currents: Indigenous Nguni and Sotho-Tswana grain, legume, wild-greens, and fermented-milk traditions (pap, phuthu, umngqusho, morogo, amasi); Afrikaans preservation and wood-fire cookery anchored by toasted coriander and vinegar (biltong, boerewors, potjiekos); Cape Malay sweet-sour spiced stews and bakes (bobotie, bredie); and Durban Indian masala curries (bunny chow). Key scholarly and historical disputes surround the lineage of bobotie—variously traced to Indonesian bobotok brought by enslaved Southeast Asian cooks under Dutch East India Company rule or to European and Roman Apicius custard-topped meat precursors, with scholars such as Gabeba Baderoon emphasizing its creolized development under Cape slavery—and competing oral histories of Durban's bunny chow (Grey Street Bania merchants, Royal Durban Golf Club caddies, or sugar-plantation workers under racial segregation), alongside strict South African statutory definitions separating 90%-meat boerewors from commercial filler sausages. ## East African (`east_african`)
- **Subtitle:** Ugali, nyama choma, tamarind, coconut pilau
- **Summary & Disputes:** Kenyan and Tanzanian home cooking bridges two broad culinary systems: inland agro-pastoralist meals built around maize staples (ugali, githeri), braised leafy greens (sukuma wiki) and charcoal-roasted meats (nyama choma), and Swahili coastal cooking shaped by over a millennium of Indian Ocean dhow trade using two-stage coconut milk, tamarind and Zanzibar spices in dishes like pilau, biryani, mishkaki and mahamri. Culinary historians such as James C. McCann and Igor Cusack highlight how Columbian Exchange crops (maize, cassava and chiles) largely displaced indigenous sorghum and millet in everyday staples, while Swahili food scholarship debates how to weigh Omani Arab and South Asian influences alongside foundational Bantu agricultural practices and the domestic expertise of coastal women (Wamama). Common ingredients also carry distinct regional names across Kenya and Tanzania—such as dhania versus giligilani for cilantro, omena versus dagaa for dried Lake Victoria silver cyprinid fish, and pilipili mbuzi for Scotch-bonnet-type chiles versus pilipili kichaa for bird's-eye chiles. ## Ghanaian (`ghanaian`)

Key regional and cultural distinctions include the Ghana-versus-Nigeria jollof debate—where Ghanaian cooks usually prefer fragrant jasmine or basmati rice cooked in a ginger-garlic-onion tomato stew rather than parboiled long-grain rice—as well as north-south divides between grain-based and tuber/plantain-based staples and differences between corn-husk-wrapped Ga kenkey and plantain-leaf-wrapped Fante kenkey. ## Central African (`central_african`)
- **Subtitle:** Palm butter, cassava leaves, njangsang
- **Summary & Disputes:** Central African rainforest and Congo Basin cooking is anchored in unrefined red palm oil and palm nut pulp (moambe/nyembwe), cassava roots and leaves (fufu, chikwangue/bobolo, pondu/saka-saka), peanuts, smoked fish, and leaf-wrapped steaming (liboké), while displaying strong sub-regional identities: Cameroonian kitchens emphasize bitterleaf ndolé, njangsang, country onion (rondelle), Penja pepper, and charred mbongo spice; Gabonese cooking centers nyembwe and odika (wild mango seed paste); and Congolese cooking highlights pondu, fumbua, and makayabu. Scholarly and culinary disputes center on frequent cross-border confusion of spice names (especially mistaking country onion, Afrostyrax lepidophyllus, for calabash nutmeg, Monodora myristica, or equating Gabonese odika sauces with Nigerian ogbono soup), as well as variation over whether pondu and moambe should remain pure palm preparations or be enriched with peanut butter. LOW CONFIDENCE applies to sodium estimates for commercial bouillon cubes (which vary widely by brand and size, often supplying about 900 to over 1,000 mg sodium per cube, or roughly 2.2 to 2.5 g salt) and canned palm nut creams (ranging from near-zero salt in plain pulp to heavily salted prepared soup bases), as well as exact simmer times across fresh, frozen pre-blanched, and substitute greens.
- **Pantry (10):** unrefined red palm oil, neutral vegetable oil, onions, garlic and fresh ginger, leeks or scallions, hot chiles (pili-pili or habanero), tomato paste, unsweetened peanut butter, commercial bouillon cubes, ground dried crayfish, cassava flour (fufu) or white rice

## Native American (`us_native_american`)
- **Subtitle:** Three Sisters, wild rice, cedar smoke
- **Summary & Disputes:** Native American cuisine spans distinct regional North American ecosystems—Woodland and Southwestern Three Sisters agriculture (nixtamalized corn, beans, squash), Great Lakes hand-harvested manoomin (wild rice), Pacific Northwest cedar-planked and alder-smoked salmon, berries, camas, and eulachon grease, and Southwestern chiles, tepary beans, and juniper-ash blue corn—built around natural aromatics like sumac, wild bergamot, ramps, sassafras filé, maple, and wood smoke rather than dairy, wheat, or refined sugar. The central scholarly and community debate contrasts strictly pre-colonial "decolonized" cooking (championed by food sovereignty leaders like Sean Sherman and NATIFS, omitting wheat flour, dairy, beef, pork, and cane sugar) against post-reservation survival traditions such as frybread and commodity-era stews, which many communities embrace as symbols of resilience while others critique for their ties to forced relocation and diet-related chronic illness; scholars also warn against pan-Indigenous homogenization that blends distant regional ingredients. Sodium and salt figures in the shortcuts section carry LOW CONFIDENCE because commercial canned hominy, jarred red chile sauces, boxed seasoned wild rice mixes, packaged frybread mixes, and blended liquid smokes vary substantially by formulation.

## Pacific Northwest (`us_pacific_northwest`)
- **Subtitle:** Cedar smoke, hazelnuts, shoyu, mushrooms
- **Summary & Disputes:** Washington and Oregon home cooking blends Indigenous Coast Salish and Plateau First Foods (wild salmon, shellfish, native berries), pioneer orchard, dairy, and nut agriculture (apples, pears, hazelnuts, marionberries, Walla Walla sweet onions), Nordic preservation traditions, and deep Asian-Pacific coastal influences (Seattle teriyaki, miso, soy, ginger) into lightly seasoned, seasonal meals. Scholarly and regional debates center on whether "Northwest cuisine" was a late-20th-century chef-and-media construction (as analyzed in Amy Jo Woodruff's thesis on regional identity) rather than a long-settled folk canon, whether Cascadian food boundaries properly include British Columbia and Alaska, and how everyday immigrant staples like Seattle teriyaki sit beside restaurant-led foraging and modern cedar-plank adaptations of Indigenous open-fire stake-roasting. Exact sodium and salt figures for commercial bottled teriyaki sauces, miso pastes, alder-smoked rubs, flaky smoked sea salts, and bottled clam juice are marked LOW CONFIDENCE because recipes, fermentation times, and flake crystal densities vary substantially by brand.

## Great Lakes (`us_great_lakes`)
- **Subtitle:** Walleye, wild rice, rye, dill, dairy
- **Summary & Disputes:** Great Lakes and Upper Midwest cooking (Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Michigan) merges Anishinaabe and Dakota lake-and-forest foodways—notably hand-harvested wild rice (manoomin), lake whitefish, walleye, maple sugar, and foraged berries—with 19th- and early 20th-century Cornish, Scandinavian, German, Polish, Finnish, and Belgian preservation traditions built on smoking, curing, pickling, rye, caraway, dill, mustard, and farmstead dairy. Key scholarly and regional disputes center on the ecological and cultural divide between Indigenous hand-harvested lake manoomin and industrial paddy-grown wild rice; the Upper Peninsula pasty debate over rutabaga versus carrots and brown gravy versus ketchup; whether mid-century canned-soup hotdish stereotypes overshadow scratch lake-and-farmstead cooking; and hyper-local specialties such as northeastern Wisconsin Belgian booyah, Marquette County warm-spiced cudighi, and Door County and Northwest Michigan Montmorency tart cherry traditions. Salt estimates for commercial fish-fry mixes, bouillon cubes, prepared horseradish, and jarred tartar sauce are marked LOW CONFIDENCE because sodium levels vary substantially across brands and formulations.

## Appalachian (`us_appalachian`)
- **Subtitle:** Soup beans, sorghum, ramps, cornmeal
- **Summary & Disputes:** Blue Ridge and Cumberland Appalachian cooking is a resourceful, preservation-centered mountain cuisine built on Indigenous Three Sisters agriculture and woodland foraging, European settler curing and baking, and African American and Affrilachian culinary traditions, alongside localized coal- and timber-camp foods in northern and central counties. Key scholarly and regional disputes center on the insistence on unsweetened stone-ground white cornmeal over sugar-added or roller-milled yellow cornbread, the common mislabeling of sweet sorghum grass syrup as cane "molasses," subregional boundaries between southern cove cooking and West Virginia traditions like pepperoni rolls and buckwheat cakes, and correcting monolithic white pioneer myths to recognize Cherokee and Affrilachian roots. Sodium estimates for commercial shortcuts such as self-rising flour or cornmeal, ham bases, and jarred chow-chow are LOW CONFIDENCE because formulations vary substantially by brand, so cooks should check label sodium and convert using about 2.5 g salt per 1,000 mg sodium.
- **Pantry (9):** stone-ground white cornmeal, all-purpose or self-rising flour, dried pinto or heirloom beans, buttermilk, bacon drippings or lard, yellow onions and garlic, apple cider vinegar, sorghum syrup, apple butter
