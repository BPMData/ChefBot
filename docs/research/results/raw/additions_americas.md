## 1. Summary of Biggest Surprises, Regional Splits, and Disputes

* **`us_new_mexican` (New Mexico vs. Tex-Mex and Mexican):**
  * **Biggest surprise:** New Mexican cooking is **not** a subset of Tex-Mex or central Mexican cuisine. Isolated along the upper Rio Grande since 1598, it blends Indigenous Pueblo/Navajo/Apache agriculture (blue corn, *quelites*, juniper-ash nixtamalization) with Spanish colonial habits and Route 66 diner staples.
  * **Key differences:** Unlike Tex-Mex, traditional New Mexican red and green chile sauces usually skip cumin, commercial chili-powder blends, pickled jalapeños, and Velveeta-style queso, relying instead on pure roasted green or sun-dried red *Capsicum annuum* pods thickened with a light lard-and-flour roux, stacked flat corn enchiladas (often topped with a fried egg), and giant puffed *sopaipillas* with honey as table bread. Unlike central/southern Mexican cooking, it defaults to whole or lard-cooked pinto beans (over black beans), everyday wheat-flour tortillas alongside blue corn, yellow Cheddar/Monterey Jack, *posole* (often from un-popped dried kernels), and New Mexico pods rather than poblano/tomatillo/*epazote*.
  * **Hatch vs. Chimayó & "Christmas":** **Hatch** (southern Rio Grande valley) is a geographic origin famous primarily for flame-roasted **green chile** (picked unripe Aug–Oct from cultivars launched by NMSU's Fabián García in 1913, later *Big Jim* and *Sandia*). **Chimayó** (northern foothills) is an heirloom landrace traditionally left to ripen **red**, sun-dried in *ristras*, and prized for its earthy, subtly sweet complexity. **"Christmas"** (coined in Santa Fe restaurants in the 1980s) is the everyday answer to New Mexico's official state question *"Red or green?"*, meaning half red chile sauce and half green chile sauce on the same dish.
  * **Dispute:** Purists insist traditional northern red chile contains only dried pods, garlic, salt, water/stock, and sometimes Mexican oregano (no cumin, no tomato), whereas southern New Mexico and Albuquerque diner versions of green chile stew/sauce often include tomatoes and occasional cumin in ground-beef fillings.

* **`us_hawaiian` (Local Food vs. Native Hawaiian & Tourist Clichés):**
  * **App default:** Following culinary historian Rachel Laudan and UH Mānoa scholarship, **the app should default to contemporary Hawaii "local food"** (plate lunches, *shoyu* or *ʻinamona* *poke*, *kālua* pork, *loco moco*, *saimin*, Spam *musubi*) because that is what residents cook and eat daily—while **explicitly tagging Native Hawaiian (*Kānaka ʻŌiwi*) dishes** (*poi*, *kalo*, *laulau*, *lūʻau*, *heʻe*) separately so settler-plantation cooking is not mislabeled as Indigenous.
  * **Five-way plantation fusion:** Local food grew out of 19th–20th-century sugar and pineapple plantation camps plus WWII military rations, merging Hawaiian earth-oven roasting with **Japanese** (*shōyu*, *teriyaki*, *katsu*, *furikake*), **Chinese** (*char siu*, *manapua*, *saimin*), **Filipino** (*adobo*, *patis*, *bagoong*), **Korean** (*kalbi*, *meat jun*, *kimchi*), and **Portuguese** (*malasadas*, *linguiça*-style sausage, *vinha d'alhos*), anchored by two scoops of sticky rice and mayonnaise-heavy macaroni salad.
  * **Tourist-menu clichés:** Pineapple chunks or juice on savory meats/poke are a mainland trope rooted in 20th-century Dole cannery marketing, **not** traditional Hawaiian or everyday local seasoning; **"Hawaiian pizza"** (ham and pineapple) was invented in **Chatham, Ontario, Canada in 1962** by Sam Panopoulos. Mainland fast-casual "poke bowls" buried under quinoa, kale, edamame, and mango also bear little resemblance to Hawaii poke. Coconut milk (*waiū niu*) is used primarily in desserts (*haupia*, *kūlolo*) rather than savory dishes.

* **`quebecois` (Three Culinary Tiers & French Divergence):**
  * **Three distinct tiers that must stay separate:** (1) **Everyday home cooking**—practical winter and working-class comfort dishes (*soupe aux pois*, *pâté chinois*, *cretons*, *ragoût de boulettes*, *bouilli*); (2) **Cabane à sucre (sugar shack)**—seasonal March–April maple-harvest feasts (*fèves au lard*, *oreilles de crisse*, *jambon à l'érable*, *tire sur la neige*) that are not weekly home dinners; and (3) **Casse-croûte & modern restaurant terroir**—1950s rural diner fast food (*poutine*, *steamies*, *hot chicken*), Montreal Jewish-immigrant icons (*smoked meat*, wood-fired *bagels*), and contemporary boreal-foraged fine dining (*poivre des dunes*, cloudberries).
  * **How it differs from French cooking:** Rooted in 17th-century northwestern France (Poitou, Normandy) and cut off from French wine imports after the 1760 British Conquest, traditional Quebec home cooking rarely uses wine reductions or classical Parisian cream sauces. Instead, it thickens stews with **dry-toasted flour (*farine grillée*)**, pairs savory pork and pies with **warm baking spices (clove, cinnamon, nutmeg, allspice) and summer savory (*sarriette*)**, integrates **maple syrup and molasses** from Indigenous *acériculture* and Caribbean trade, and reflects British/Scottish/Irish baking and potato casseroles (*pâté chinois*, *sucre à la crème*).
  * **Dispute:** *Tourtière* means a shallow spiced ground-pork/beef/veal pie in Montreal and western Quebec, whereas in Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean that pie is called *pâté à la viande* and *tourtière* (originally also *cipaille*) refers to a deep, 6–8-hour slow-baked casserole of cubed meats, game, and cubed potatoes.

* **`colombian` (Extreme Regional Divergence & App Default):**
  * **No single Colombian table:** Colombia's Ministry of Culture stresses that Colombia has multiple distinct regional cuisines rather than one unified national style: **Andean highlands** (potatoes, corn, *fríjoles*, pork/chicken, *hogao*, *guascas* in *ajiaco*), **Caribbean coast** (coconut rice with *titoté*, fried *mojarra*, *posta negra*, yam *mote de queso*, *suero costeño*, Arab *kibbeh*), **Pacific coast** (Afro-Colombian/Indigenous *encocado* seafood stews seasoned with *hierbas de azotea*—*chillangua*, *chirarán*, *poleo*), and **Llanos Orientales** (open-fire pit-roasted beef *mamona*).
  * **App default:** Treat **Andean interior home cooking** (*hogao* of *cebolla larga* + tomato + cumin + *achiote*, cilantro, *panela*, rice, *fríjoles*, plantains, and soups like *sancocho*, *sudado*, and *ajiaco*) as the default everyday baseline, while branching explicitly to coconut/*suero*/*hierbas de azotea* when the user targets Caribbean or Pacific dishes.
  * **Biggest surprise:** Classic Colombian cooking is **mild, not spicy** (heat sits strictly in optional tabletop *ají* sauce), and Colombian *natilla* is an **eggless** cornstarch-and-*panela* pudding rather than Spanish egg custard.
